Quantum and Aum: Why I'm Starting This Journey

One word from a physics lab, one from the Upanishads, and a promise about how I'll explore the space between them.

By Deva · 7 November 2026 · 8 min read

In December 1900, a German physicist named Max Planck presented a strange idea he didn't fully believe himself. To explain how hot objects glow, he had to assume that energy isn't released in a smooth, continuous flow, but in tiny, separate packets. He called each packet a quantum, from the Latin for "how much." That small, reluctant assumption grew into quantum physics, the most accurate theory humanity has ever built and also the strangest.

Thousands of years earlier, the sages of the Upanishads were looking for the smallest, deepest truth too, from the other direction. They didn't look outward through instruments. They looked inward through attention. What they found, they gave a sound: Aum (ॐ).

This site joins those two words: Quant-aum. In this first post I want to explain why, what you can expect here, and the one promise I'm making to you and to myself.

Why put these two words together?

I grew up with stories: Shiva's dance, Sati and Parvati, the churning of the ocean. Later I studied computer science and spent my career in a world of data, systems and evidence. For a long time those felt like two separate rooms in my head. One held meaning, the other held facts.

The more I read, the more I noticed something. The deepest questions in physics and the deepest questions in Advaita Vedanta sound oddly alike. What is the world really made of? Is the observer separate from what is observed? Can something that seems to vanish truly be lost? Scientists and rishis used completely different methods, but they kept arriving at the same doorways.

Quant-aum is my attempt to stand in those doorways and describe what I see, as simply as I can.

The promise: resonance, not proof

Before going further, I want to be honest, because this space is full of exaggeration. You've probably seen videos claiming that "the Vedas already knew quantum mechanics" or that "science has proven consciousness creates reality." I won't make claims like that.

My promise: On Quant-aum I'll tell you clearly when I'm stating established science, when I'm sharing what a scripture says, and when I'm drawing an analogy of my own. An analogy can be beautiful and illuminating without being a proof. Physics doesn't need the Upanishads to be valid, and the Upanishads don't need physics to be valid. Each is richer for being read next to the other.

With that promise made, here are three resonances that pulled me into this journey. Each will get its own deep-dive post, podcast episode and short video later.

Three resonances to begin with

1. One field, many forms

In modern physics, an electron isn't really a tiny ball. According to quantum field theory, there is a single electron field spread through all of space, and every electron in the universe is a ripple, or excitation, in that one field. That's why every electron is perfectly identical to every other: they are all expressions of the same underlying thing.

Advaita says something with a similar shape. There is one reality, Brahman, and the countless things we see are nāma-rūpa, names and forms appearing on it. The classic image is gold and jewellery: ring, bangle and chain look different and have different names, but they are all gold.

PhysicsMany particles, one underlying field. Each electron is an excitation of the same electron field.
AdvaitaMany forms, one underlying reality. Each form is a name and shape appearing on Brahman.

The analogy has limits. Physics has many fields, not one, and a field is a mathematical object, not consciousness. But the pattern of "underlying unity, apparent multiplicity" shows up in both, and I find that worth sitting with.

2. The observer and the observed

One of the most debated puzzles in quantum physics is the measurement problem. Before a measurement, a quantum system is described as a spread of possibilities. When we measure it, we find one definite result. What exactly happens in between is still argued over by physicists today.

A short Advaita text called the Dṛg-Dṛśya-Viveka, "discerning the seer from the seen," opens with a clean step-by-step analysis. The eye sees forms, the mind sees the eye, and awareness sees the mind. Awareness itself is never seen; it is the final witness, the sākṣī.

Here I need to apply my promise carefully. In physics, "observer" usually means any measuring interaction, such as a detector, a photon or a molecule of air. It does not have to mean a conscious human. So quantum mechanics does not prove that consciousness creates reality. What both traditions share is a refusal to take the relationship between knower and known for granted. That question will run through a lot of what I write.

3. Energy that changes form but is never lost

This is the story that started it all for me.

Sati, daughter of Daksha and wife of Shiva, gives up her body in the fire of her father's yajña after he insults Shiva. It looks like an ending. But she is born again as Parvati, daughter of the mountain king Himavan. Through long tapas she returns to Shiva. What seemed destroyed was transformed.

Physics has a law with exactly this shape: the first law of thermodynamics, or conservation of energy. Energy can't be created or destroyed, only changed from one form into another: chemical into heat, heat into motion, motion into electricity. Nothing is lost. It is only moved.

"It is never born, and it never dies. Having been, it does not cease to be. Unborn, eternal, ancient, it is not killed when the body is killed."Bhagavad Gita 2.20 (my rendering)

The Gita says this about the Self, not about joules. Mixing the two would be a mistake. But the intuition that what is essential is transformed rather than destroyed appears in both the story of Sati and the laws of physics. I think that's why the story feels true even to people who don't take it literally.

Why Aum, and not some other word?

The Māṇḍūkya Upanishad, only twelve verses long, treats Aum as a map of consciousness. Its three sounds, A, U and M, stand for waking, dreaming and deep sleep. The silence after the syllable stands for turīya, "the fourth," pure awareness underlying all three.

I love that the most sacred symbol in this tradition is a vibration that fades into silence. Physics, in its own language, describes the universe as fields vibrating, with a quiet ground state underneath. I'm not claiming these are the same thing. But you can see why a person with one foot in each world might end up naming his blog Quant-aum.

I'm not writing as a physicist or a swami. I'm writing as a curious learner who wants to understand both, and who believes the best way to learn something is to explain it simply to someone else. Thank you for being that someone.
