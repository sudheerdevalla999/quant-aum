In the great temple of Chidambaram in Tamil Nadu, Shiva is worshipped in five elemental forms across five temples, and at Chidambaram the element is *ākāśa*, space. Behind a curtain in the sanctum lies what devotees call the *Chidambara Rahasyam*, the secret of Chidambaram. When the curtain is drawn back, there is nothing there. Just a space, hung with a garland of golden leaves.

The holiest image in that temple is an empty space. And the same temple is home to Nataraja, Shiva as the cosmic dancer, the very picture of movement.

Stillness and dance, in one place. That pairing is the subject of this essay.

## Shiva the still, Shakti the moving

Across the Shaiva and Shakta traditions there is a recurring way of describing reality in two aspects:

- **Shiva** is the still, unchanging ground: pure awareness, silent, like space itself. Think of Dakshinamurti, Shiva as the teacher, who teaches his disciples not with words but with silence.
- **Shakti**, embodied as Parvati, is the power that moves: energy, vibration, the creative pulse that becomes everything we see.

The two are never really apart. Ardhanarishvara, Shiva who is half Parvati, shows them in one body.

The most striking statement of this comes in the very first verse of the *Saundarya Lahari*, a hymn traditionally attributed to Adi Shankara. In my own rendering:

> Only when Shiva is united with Shakti is he able to create. Without her, the god cannot even stir.
>
> — *Saundarya Lahari, verse 1*

The Sanskrit word for "stir" there is *spanditum*, from *spanda*, which means pulsation or vibration. In Kashmir Shaivism, *spanda* became a whole philosophy: the universe as the subtle, ceaseless throb of consciousness. Stillness and vibration, Shiva and Shakti, are two faces of one reality.

So here is the question I want to explore. Is there anything in modern physics with that shape: a still ground that is never truly empty, and a vibration that is never separate from it?

There is, in at least four places.

## 1. Empty space is not empty

For centuries, "empty space" meant exactly that: a container with nothing in it. Quantum physics changed that completely.

According to quantum field theory, even a perfect vacuum, with every particle removed and cooled as far as possible, is not *nothing*. It is the lowest-energy state of fields that fill all of space. And because of the uncertainty principle, those fields can never be perfectly still. Even at their quietest, they keep a tiny, irreducible jitter called **zero-point energy**.

This isn't just theory. In 1948 the Dutch physicist Hendrik Casimir predicted that two metal plates placed extremely close together in a vacuum would be pushed towards each other by these vacuum fluctuations. The **Casimir effect** has since been measured in the laboratory.

## 2. Everything that moves is a vibration of something still

In [my first essay]({% post_url 2026-11-07-quantum-and-aum %}) I described how every electron in the universe is a ripple in one underlying electron field. The same is true of every particle: photons are ripples in the electromagnetic field, quarks in quark fields, and so on.

So when physicists describe the universe, they describe it as fields spread through space, with all the "stuff" we see being their excitations: their waves and vibrations. The field in its quiet ground state is the backdrop; the vibrations on it are the world.

This is remarkably close to the picture of Nataraja: the dance does not happen *in addition to* the still ground; the dance is the ground in motion. India gifted a two-metre statue of Nataraja to CERN, the world's largest particle physics laboratory, where it was unveiled on 18 June 2004. A plaque beside it uses the dance as a metaphor for the "cosmic dance" of subatomic particles that physicists study there.

## 3. Space and energy shape each other

Einstein's general theory of relativity changed what space itself is. Space (more precisely, spacetime) is no longer a passive stage. Matter and energy curve spacetime, and the curvature of spacetime tells matter and energy how to move. The physicist John Wheeler summed it up in one famous line, roughly: spacetime tells matter how to move; matter tells spacetime how to curve.

Neither one acts alone. Neither is the master. They define each other.

That is Ardhanarishvara in the language of physics. Shiva without Shakti cannot stir; Shakti has nowhere to dance without Shiva. Space without energy is inert; energy without space has no "where" to exist. The tradition insists on a relationship of mutual dependence, not hierarchy, and so, in its own way, does relativity.

## 4. Even absolute zero is not still

Here is my favourite example, because it is so physical.

Cool anything down and its atoms slow down. At absolute zero (−273.15 °C), classical physics says all motion would stop completely: perfect stillness.

But nature doesn't allow it. Quantum mechanics says that particles can never sit perfectly still at an exact point, so even at absolute zero they keep that zero-point motion. Helium shows this dramatically. At normal pressure, helium *never freezes*. It stays liquid even at absolute zero, because its zero-point jiggle is too strong for the atoms to lock into a solid. You have to squeeze it at about 25 times normal atmospheric pressure to make it solid.

Perfect stillness, it turns out, is a limit nature approaches but never reaches. There is always a residual pulse. The rishis might have smiled at that.

## Where the analogy breaks

<div class="note"><strong>Keeping my promise:</strong> In the tradition, Shiva is <em>consciousness</em>, the aware witness, and Shakti is its conscious power. The quantum vacuum, fields and spacetime are not conscious; they are physical things described by mathematics. Physics does not say that space is awareness, and nothing in this essay claims it does. A second caution: in the nineteenth century physicists also believed in a still, invisible medium filling space, the "luminiferous aether", through which light waves travelled. Experiments, most famously by Michelson and Morley in 1887, found no trace of it. So "a still medium behind all vibration" is not automatically good physics; the modern picture of fields is subtler. And a third: Advaita Vedanta, Kashmir Shaivism and Shakta traditions don't describe Shiva and Shakti in exactly the same way. Advaita in particular treats the moving world as appearance on the one changeless reality. What I am pointing at is a shared <em>pattern</em>: the still and the moving are inseparable, and the deepest stillness is never empty.</div>

## Why stillness matters to you and me

If you've ever sat quietly for a few minutes, you'll know this from the inside. You try to make the mind still, and you discover it is anything but: thoughts arise, sensations flicker, the breath moves. Stillness is never empty. There is always a pulse.

Meditation traditions don't ask you to stop that pulse. They invite you to notice the awareness in which it all moves: the space in which the dance happens. Shiva and Shakti, in that sense, are not only cosmic. They are the silent witness and the moving mind, right here.

Physics found that empty space hums. The rishis seem to have found that silence pulses. Neither proves the other. But sitting with both, I find it a little easier to understand why Chidambaram keeps an empty space at its heart, and a dancer right beside it.
