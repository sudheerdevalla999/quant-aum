Sati, Parvati and the Conservation of Energy

A goddess whose name means energy disappears into fire and returns as the daughter of a mountain. Physics has a law with exactly that shape.

By Sudheer Devalla · 7 November 2026 · 10 min read

Start with a single word: Shakti. In Sanskrit it means power, capacity, energy. In the Indian tradition it is also the name of the Goddess herself, the living energy of the universe, inseparable from Shiva just as heat is inseparable from fire.

So when the Puranas tell the story of Sati, an embodiment of Shakti, who gives up her body and returns in a new form, they are telling a story about what happens to energy. In this post I want to read that story slowly, next to the most trusted law in all of physics, and see what each can show us about the other.

The story

Sati was the daughter of Daksha, a powerful Prajapati. She loved Shiva, the ash-smeared ascetic of the cremation grounds, and married him against her father's wishes. Daksha never forgave either of them.

Years later, Daksha held a grand yajña and invited every god except Shiva. Sati went anyway, hoping to make peace. Instead, in front of the whole assembly, her father mocked her husband. Sati would not keep a body that had come from such a man. In many tellings she sat in meditation and called up an inner, yogic fire, and her body was consumed by it.

What followed shook the cosmos. Shiva's grief turned to fury. From his matted hair came Virabhadra, who destroyed the sacrifice. Then Shiva lifted Sati's body and wandered the universe with it, lost in sorrow, his dance threatening to undo creation. To end it, Vishnu's discus parted the body into pieces, and wherever each piece fell, the earth became sacred. These are the Shakti Peethas, seats of the Goddess across the subcontinent.

But the story does not end there. Sati was born again as Parvati, "she of the mountain," daughter of Himavan, king of the Himalaya. Shiva, withdrawn into deep meditation, did not notice her. Even Kamadeva's arrow only earned the god of desire a burning from Shiva's third eye. So Parvati did what no one expected. She undertook tapas, years of austerity so intense that it shook the heavens. Finally Shiva came to her, and Shakti and Shiva were united once again.

The law

Now step into a physics classroom. The first law of thermodynamics, better known as the conservation of energy, says this:

Energy can neither be created nor destroyed. It can only change from one form into another.The first law of thermodynamics

Burn a log and its chemical energy becomes heat and light. Drop a ball and gravitational energy becomes motion, then a little sound and warmth when it lands. Add up every form of energy before and after any event, and the totals always match. Nothing is lost. Nothing appears from nowhere. Things only change form.

This law was pieced together in the 1840s by scientists such as James Joule, who showed that mechanical work and heat are two forms of the same thing, and Hermann von Helmholtz, who stated the principle in its general form. In 1918 the mathematician Emmy Noether showed something even deeper. Energy is conserved because the laws of physics do not change over time. The rules that held yesterday hold today, and that steadiness is exactly why the total energy stays constant.

Four places where the story and the law rhyme

1. Disappearance is not destruction

When Sati enters the fire, it looks like an ending. The gods despair and Shiva is undone. Yet the story insists that she has not ceased to exist. Her energy has gone into a form we can't yet see, and it reappears as Parvati.

Physics teaches the same humility about appearances. When a moving car brakes to a stop, its energy of motion seems to "disappear." It hasn't. It has become heat in the brakes and the road. What our eyes call loss is very often a change of form we haven't noticed.

PhysicsThe car stops. Its kinetic energy becomes heat. The total is unchanged.
StorySati leaves her body in fire. Her Shakti returns as Parvati. The Goddess is unchanged.

2. The Shakti Peethas: energy that spreads out

This is the detail I find most striking. Sati's body isn't simply gone. It's distributed across the land, and every place a piece touches becomes charged with sacredness.

That is how energy behaves when it seems to vanish: it spreads out. The heat from the braking car warms the air molecules around it, ever so slightly, across a wide area. Physicists call this dissipation. The energy is all still there, just scattered so thinly that we stop noticing it. The Shakti Peethas are a beautiful image of that idea. The energy is everywhere now, quietly present in the ground beneath our feet.

3. Why Parvati needed tapas

Here physics adds a twist that makes the story even richer. The second law of thermodynamics says that although energy is never lost, it naturally becomes less organised and less useful over time. Heat spreads out, and it never gathers itself back together on its own. To concentrate scattered energy into a useful form again, you have to put in work.

Look at Parvati. Sati's Shakti is not lost. It lives on in her. But it doesn't automatically flow back into union with Shiva. That reunion has to be earned through tapas, a word that literally means "heat" or "burning." Tapas is concentrated effort that gathers scattered energy into a single focused purpose. The story seems to know what the second law says: conservation guarantees that nothing is lost, but bringing it back together takes work.

4. Why anything is conserved at all

Remember Noether's discovery: energy is conserved because the laws of nature don't change with time. Conservation flows from something constant underneath.

In the tradition, Sati's return is also grounded in something unchanging: the eternal bond of Shiva and Shakti, consciousness and energy, which no insult, fire or death can break. Forms change, from Sati to Parvati and from daughter of Daksha to daughter of the mountain, because the underlying relationship does not. Different domains, but the same deep shape: change on the surface, constancy underneath.

Where the analogy breaks

Keeping my promise: In physics, "energy" is a precisely measured quantity in joules. In the tradition, Shakti is the conscious, living power of the Divine. They are not the same thing, and the Puranas were not describing thermodynamics. Physics also has no rule that energy "remembers" who it was. The heat from Sati's fire would not reassemble into a particular person. The story claims a continuity of identity that physics doesn't address at all. What the two share is a pattern: things that seem destroyed are transformed, scattered things can be gathered again with effort, and change rests on something that doesn't change. That pattern is worth noticing, and it doesn't need to be a proof to be meaningful.

Why this matters outside a classroom

I think we tell this story for the same reason physicists trust their law. Both refuse to accept loss as the final word.

Everyone eventually faces a "Sati moment": something precious that seems to vanish into fire, whether a relationship, a career, a version of ourselves we loved. Shiva's response is the human one: grief so large it threatens to stop the world. The story doesn't scold him for it. But it also shows what comes next. The energy that seemed lost is still there, scattered, waiting. Gathering it back takes tapas: patience, discipline, focused effort, sometimes for years.

The first law tells us nothing is lost. The second law tells us that rebuilding takes work. Parvati's story tells us the same thing in a language that reaches the heart. Read together, they give us something neither gives alone: the certainty that nothing essential is destroyed, and the honesty that putting it back together is up to us.

Next time, we'll go further into the idea at the centre of all this: Shiva and Shakti, and why the tradition insists that consciousness and energy can never really be separated.
